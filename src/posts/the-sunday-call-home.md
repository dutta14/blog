My mother's face fills the entire screen when she video calls on WhatsApp, her forehead cut off at the top, her chin at the bottom, as if the phone is something she is trying to look through rather than at. Behind her I can see the living room in Jamshedpur, the sofa with the brown cover that used to be a different brown, the framed photo of my grandparents on the shelf that has moved slightly to the left since the last call. She is saying something about the weather, and I am standing in my kitchen in Redmond, Sunday morning, coffee cooling on the counter, watching her mouth move half a second before her voice arrives.

The call has been happening every Sunday for ten years, give or take the Sundays I was traveling or the Sundays she forgot because something was on television. In the beginning, when I was at USC, the calls were short and factual. How are classes. Am I eating. Do I need money. The answer to the last one was always no, even when it should have been yes, and she knew this, and we both let it pass without comment. My father would appear in the background, sometimes leaning into the frame to say one thing, then leaving to watch cricket. His participation was always brief and specific, like a man who had said what he came to say.

The shape of the call changed when I got the job at Amazon. Suddenly there were things to explain. What is an SDE. What does Level 5 mean. Why Seattle, which is far from Los Angeles, which is already far from Jamshedpur. My mother asked these questions with genuine interest and a quiet kind of worry that lived in the distance the answer implied, more than in the answer itself. She understood that I was doing well. She understood it from the outside, from the job title and the company name and the fact that I had bought a car, which in India carries a slightly different weight than it does here.

Later, the calls became about the things they did not say. My father's knee surgery, which I learned about after it happened because they did not want me to worry. The monsoon flooding in the neighborhood, mentioned casually two weeks later. My mother's blood pressure, which she discusses only when I ask directly and only then with a tone that suggests I am overreacting. I started reading the silences the way you read whitespace in code, looking for what had been left out on purpose.

Then I left Microsoft for Meta, and the calls changed again. My mother did not understand why someone would leave Microsoft. Microsoft was a name she could say to relatives. It carried weight at dinner parties in Jamshedpur. Meta was Facebook, which was something she used to post photos of pujas, and she could not connect the thing she used with the thing I was now doing for a living. I explained it several times. She said she understood. I think what she understood was that her son made a decision she would not have made, and she was deciding to trust it anyway.

The calls now are quieter. My parents are older, and the quiet is partly the comfort of a long habit and partly the weight of things we have agreed, without agreeing, not to talk about. How often I visit. Whether I will come back. How long this life in America is going to go on being the life. I do not have answers to these questions. My mother does not ask them directly. She asks whether I have eaten, whether the weather is nice, whether I am sleeping enough. The real questions live underneath, carried by the pause after I answer, the half-second where she looks at the screen with an expression I can read from eight thousand miles away.

I call every Sunday. It is the easiest and hardest thing I do all week.
