The US consulate in Delhi has plastic chairs arranged in rows, the kind you find in hospital waiting rooms, and a number display above the window counter that moves forward with no discernible pattern. I was holding a brown envelope I had been told not to open. The man sitting next to me was holding the same kind of envelope. We did not speak. The air conditioning was working too well and I was wearing a shirt I had ironed that morning, sitting very straight, as if posture mattered to the visa officer behind the glass.

The visa went on page seven of my passport. I got it back the next week, picked it up from the VFS counter in a plastic sleeve, and counted the pages standing outside on the pavement in the August heat, confirming it was real. The stamp came later, at the port of entry, a small rectangular ink mark from an officer at LAX who asked me two questions and waved me through. F-1. Student. Valid for the duration of my studies, which meant valid until it wasn't, which meant the clock started the moment I entered.

Nobody tells you this at orientation. They tell you about campus resources and health insurance and where to find free food at department talks. They do not mention that from the moment you land, every career decision you make will be shaped by a piece of paper that someone else controls. Which internship to take. Which offer to accept. Whether you can afford to leave a job you have outgrown, or whether leaving means starting a transfer process that takes eight months and during which you cannot legally work if something goes wrong. These are the calculations you do in your head while appearing to evaluate an offer on its merits.

I accepted my first job at Samsung in India without thinking about any of this. I accepted my first job in America, at Amazon, thinking about almost nothing else. The offer was good. The team was interesting. The work was real. The thing that made me say yes in under a week was that they would file the visa paperwork, and they would do it on time, and the lawyers they used had a reputation for not making mistakes. I did not tell the recruiter this was the reason. I talked about the technical challenge and the team culture, and everything I said was true. The visa paperwork was simply the thing I thought about first. The years that followed had this shape to them. Every promotion, every team change, every restless stretch where I thought about leaving, passed through the same filter. Can I do this without breaking the chain? The chain being the uninterrupted sequence of valid status, employer sponsorship, filed petitions, and approved extensions that connects the stamp on page seven to the life I am living now. A gap in the chain means leaving the country. It means starting over, or trying to, from a consulate in Delhi or Hyderabad, in a plastic chair, holding an envelope, hoping.

I have colleagues who have never thought about this. They evaluate offers purely on the work, the team, the compensation, the location. They leave jobs when the job is no longer right. They take gaps between roles. They start companies on a Thursday afternoon because the idea is good and the timing feels right. I do not begrudge them this. I notice it the way you notice a door that is open for some people and only partly open for others. The chain is still unbroken. I check on it the way you check on a sleeping child, not because anything is wrong, just because you need to know it is still there.
