It was my third week as a manager, a Thursday afternoon one-on-one with D, who had been on the team for four years and knew every service, every dependency, every reason behind every strange decision in the codebase. He asked me a question about the architecture of the voice assistant, something specific about why we had chosen a particular wake-word detection model over another, and I did not know the answer. There were five seconds of silence. In those five seconds I understood something about management that no book had prepared me for.

D was watching to see what I would do with the gap. Whether I would offer a vague answer and redirect, or whether I would say the thing that felt hardest to say out loud in my third week, which was I don't know. I said I didn't know. I asked him to explain. He did, clearly and patiently, and the one-on-one ran twenty minutes over.

There is a particular dynamic that happens when you manage someone who has more technical depth than you do. They have been on the team longer. They understand the system at a level you may never reach, because your job now operates at a different altitude, one that involves roadmaps and hiring and cross-team alignment rather than the internals of a service they wrote two years ago. They know this. You know this. The question is whether both of you can be honest about it.

The mistake I almost made was trying to close the gap. In my first two weeks, I stayed up late reading the architecture documents, tracing the code, trying to reach the level of understanding that D had built over four years. I wanted to earn his respect through technical knowledge, because that was the currency I understood. It took me a few weeks to realize that the way to earn it was to be useful in ways he could not be useful to himself.

D was brilliant at building systems. He was less interested in communicating their value to people outside the team, or in thinking about how the work connected to the product roadmap two quarters out, or in having the difficult conversation with the partner team whose priorities conflicted with ours. He could do these things. He did not want to spend his time on them, and as his manager, I could take them on in a way that made his work more visible and his time more protected. That was the trade. I could not debug the voice assistant faster than he could. I could make sure the people who decided funding knew what the voice assistant did and why it mattered.

The one-on-ones with D became the most useful hour of my week. I asked questions. He explained things I would never have found in documentation. I told him what I was hearing from leadership, what was coming, what might change. We built something that I hesitate to call trust because the word sounds too clean for what it actually was, which was a slow accumulation of small moments where neither of us pretended to be something we were not. He told me once, months later, that the thing he appreciated most was that I never faked understanding. I don't know if he meant it as high praise. I took it as the highest I had received.
